# SIPC: what brokerage account protection covers

The **Securities Investor Protection Corporation** (SIPC) is a nonprofit membership corporation created by Congress in 1970. Most U.S. broker-dealers are required to be members. When a member brokerage fails and customer assets are missing, SIPC steps in to return customers' securities and cash, up to a limit. It is the brokerage-account counterpart to the FDIC's deposit insurance for banks, with one difference that matters more than any other: SIPC protects against the **failure of the brokerage**, not against a fall in the value of what the customer owns.

## What is covered

According to SIPC's own page on what it protects, coverage is up to $500,000 per customer, of which up to $250,000 may be for cash held in the account for the purpose of buying securities. The protected items are the securities themselves (stocks, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs, options and similar instruments registered with the SEC) and the cash on deposit with the broker. In a liquidation, the trustee first returns whatever customer property the firm still holds, and SIPC advances funds to cover any shortfall within the limits. In most failures the great majority of customer assets are simply transferred to another broker and the limit never comes into play.

## What is not covered

SIPC does not cover losses from a decline in the market value of an investment. An investor whose stock fell 40% has lost money, but not to a brokerage failure, and SIPC has no role. It does not cover bad advice, unsuitable recommendations or outright fraud in the investment itself, as opposed to theft of customer assets by the firm. It does not cover commodity futures, foreign exchange or fixed annuities, and it does not cover investments held outside a SIPC member, such as an unregistered investment fund. Claims that an investment is "SIPC insured" against loss are a misuse of the term, and SIPC itself warns against them.

## Brokerage failure versus investment loss

Suppose a customer holds $300,000 of ETFs and $50,000 of cash at a broker that fails, and the trustee finds the firm's records intact. The ETFs and cash are moved to a new broker and the customer is whole. Now suppose instead that the broker is sound but the customer's $300,000 of ETFs fall to $180,000 in a bear market. The $120,000 loss is an investment loss; it is the risk the customer accepted by owning the ETFs, and no protection scheme covers it. A third case, where the broker fails and $60,000 of customer securities are missing, is the one SIPC was built for: the missing $60,000 is within the limit and SIPC advances it.

## What can go wrong

The $500,000 limit is per customer per "separate capacity", so one investor can have a cash account and an IRA each protected, but two cash accounts at the same firm are combined. Balances above the limit depend on whatever excess coverage the broker has purchased privately. Options positions are protected as securities, but SIPC returns the position, not a fixed dollar value; if the position has fallen in value during the liquidation, the loss stands. And a liquidation can freeze accounts for weeks, during which a short put cannot be closed, which is a liquidity risk SIPC does not address.

## Where this shows up in OptionsScout

The app never holds customer assets and never connects to a brokerage, so SIPC coverage is not something OptionsScout provides or can affect. It appears in the Tax & Accounts page under the Learn tab, where the account-type explainer distinguishes a cash account from a margin account and notes what protection each has at a SIPC member. The Compliance agent's banned-language list includes the words of certainty most often misused in this way, in part because of the confusion this document describes: protection against a broker's failure is often mistaken for protection against the market, and the app's explanations may not encourage that reading.
