# Form 1099-B and Form 8949: what the broker reports and how option trades appear

Every sale of stock or an option in a taxable brokerage account ends up on two pieces of paper: the broker's **Form 1099-B**, which reports the trade to the investor and to the IRS, and the investor's **Form 8949**, which lists the trades on the tax return. This is education, not tax advice; rules change and individual situations differ, so confirm with a tax professional or the IRS publication cited.

## What the broker reports on Form 1099-B

For each closed position the broker reports the description of the security, the date acquired, the date sold, the **proceeds** (what the sale brought in), and, where it is required to, the **cost basis** (what the position cost), whether the gain or loss is short-term or long-term, and any wash sale loss disallowed. Brokers are required to report basis only for **covered securities**, which include stock acquired in 2011 or later and options acquired in 2014 or later. For **noncovered securities**, older holdings or positions transferred without basis information, the broker reports proceeds only and the investor supplies the basis from their own records.

## How option transactions appear

Option trades look different from stock trades:

- A **written put that expires** shows the premium as proceeds and a basis of zero, with a short-term label, because that is the gain under section 1234(b).
- A **written put bought back** shows the premium as proceeds and the buy-back cost as basis.
- A **written put that is assigned** usually does not appear as its own line at all. The broker folds the premium into the cost basis of the shares received, so it surfaces only when those shares are later sold, as a lower basis.
- A **long call sold** shows the sale proceeds and the premium paid as basis, labelled short-term or long-term by holding period. A long call that **expires** shows proceeds of zero. A long call that is **exercised** disappears into the basis of the shares bought.

Suppose a put written for a $1.20 premium expired worthless; the 1099-B line would read proceeds $120, basis $0, short-term. Had it been assigned at a $50 strike, no option line would appear, and the shares would later show a basis of $48.80 per share.

## Form 8949 and Schedule D

Form 8949 is where the investor lists each transaction, grouped by whether the broker reported basis and whether the gain is short-term or long-term, and reconciles any differences with the 1099-B, such as a wash sale adjustment across accounts the broker could not see. The totals from Form 8949 carry to **Schedule D**, which nets short-term against short-term and long-term against long-term and arrives at the figures that go on the return. Tax Topic 409 describes the netting and points to the current year's instructions for how the result is taxed; this document does not state rates.

## What can go wrong

Brokers report accurately for what they can see, but they cannot see trades in another broker's account, so wash sales and basis across accounts are the investor's job. Transferred positions often arrive as noncovered with no basis, and a missing basis overstates the gain until corrected. Option trades near year-end can be reported in a different tax year than the investor expected, since the writer's gain lands in the year the position closes, not the year the premium arrived.
