# Early withdrawal from an IRA: the additional tax and why near-term goals do not belong there

An **individual retirement arrangement (IRA)** gives up current tax on investment gains in exchange for a promise: the money is meant to stay until retirement. The tax code enforces that promise with an **additional tax on early distributions**, described in IRS Tax Topic 557. A distribution is any money taken out of the account; it is "early" when the owner is under age 59 1/2. This is education, not tax advice; rules change and individual situations differ, so confirm with a tax professional or the IRS publication cited.

## How the additional tax works

A withdrawal from a **traditional IRA** is generally included in taxable income in the year it is taken, because the contributions and growth were never taxed. If the owner is under 59 1/2, Tax Topic 557 says an additional tax, computed as a percentage of the taxable amount withdrawn, is owed on top of the ordinary income tax, unless an exception applies. The percentage is stated in the Topic and in Form 5329's instructions; this document does not repeat it. The additional tax is reported on Form 5329 with the return.

A **Roth IRA** works differently. Contributions were made with after-tax money and can be withdrawn at any time without tax or additional tax. Earnings withdrawn before the account is five years old and the owner is 59 1/2 are generally taxable and may be subject to the additional tax. The ordering rules that decide whether a withdrawal comes from contributions or earnings are in IRS Publication 590-B, which the IRS IRA page links to.

## Exceptions exist

Tax Topic 557 lists situations in which the additional tax does not apply even though the owner is under 59 1/2. They include distributions due to total and permanent disability, certain unreimbursed medical expenses, qualified higher education expenses, a first-time home purchase up to a lifetime limit, a series of substantially equal periodic payments, and distributions to a beneficiary after the owner's death. Each exception has its own conditions and some have dollar caps. The list changes from time to time; the Topic and Publication 590-B have the current version. Ordinary income tax on a traditional IRA withdrawal still applies when an exception removes the additional tax.

## A worked illustration

Suppose an investor has $8,000 in a traditional IRA and needs $8,000 for a car in eighteen months. If the money is withdrawn at age 40 with no exception, the $8,000 is added to taxable income for that year and the additional tax is owed on it as well, so the amount that reaches the car dealer is well below $8,000. Had the same $8,000 sat in a taxable brokerage or savings account, only any gain would have been taxed. The IRA's advantage, deferral, is worth little over eighteen months and its disadvantage, the exit cost, is worth a lot.

## Why near-term goals and IRAs do not mix

An IRA rewards time. Deferred or tax-free compounding over decades is the point; a short holding period captures little of it while facing the full exit cost. Money a goal needs within a few years is better matched to an account it can leave freely. This is a description of the mechanism, not a plan for any reader; the right split between accounts depends on income, other savings and the goal itself.

## What can go wrong

Taking a distribution to cover an assigned put, or to meet a margin call in another account, converts a market problem into a tax problem. Missing the 60-day deadline on an attempted rollover turns the whole amount into a distribution. Misreading the Roth five-year rule can make earnings taxable that the owner believed were free. In every case the broker will report the distribution on Form 1099-R and the IRS will see it.

## Where this shows up in OptionsScout

The Goals tab does not know which account funds a goal, so it does not set IRA balances aside for a goal whose target date falls before the user reaches 59 1/2. A reader planning a near-term goal should leave IRA money out of the starting balance they enter, for the reasons above. The app never estimates the tax itself.
