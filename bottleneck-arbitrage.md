# Bottleneck Arbitrage and Options Strategy Selection

*A ten-step process for finding the constrained layer of a big theme's supply chain, and how that fits LEAPS, PMCCs and cash-secured puts.*

> Not financial advice. This is a framework for generating and filtering candidates, not a recommendation on any position.

---

## Core idea

Bottleneck arbitrage starts with a large, well-funded theme and works down its supply chain until it finds the layer where supply cannot grow as fast as demand. The companies selling into that layer gain pricing power and volume at the same time, and that shows up in revenue and margins before most of the market is paying attention.

The edge is **structural, not viral**. Unlike consumer trends, where the signal is a product catching on, the signal here is a shortage: lead times, waitlists, rising prices and capacity that takes years to add. Shortages unwind slowly, which gives the thesis a longer runway, but they also attract new supply, which is what eventually ends it.

The cycle in brief:

**Theme → supply chain → bottleneck → signals → companies → priced in? → catalyst → position → monitor → exit.**

Most ideas will fail one of these filters, and that is the point. The process is designed to throw out weak ideas and leave the few where the trend is real, material and not yet obvious to the market.

## The process

### Step 1: Pick a major theme

Choose a large, durable trend with heavy spending behind it, such as AI, electrification, aging populations or reshoring manufacturing.

- **Test:** is real money flowing in? Look at company capital spending plans, government programs and industry forecasts.
- **Tip:** stick to themes you understand or follow anyway. Your edge comes from paying attention.

### Step 2: Map the supply chain

List everything the theme needs, layer by layer. For AI data centers:

- Chips (GPUs, memory)
- Power (generation, grid equipment, fuel cells, nuclear)
- Cooling (liquid cooling, HVAC)
- Buildings, land, networking, fiber
- Raw materials (copper, specialty gases)

Write it out as a simple tree. The less obvious layers are often where the opportunities are.

### Step 3: Find the bottleneck

For each layer, ask whether supply can grow as fast as demand. Signs of a bottleneck:

- Long lead times or waitlists ("sold out through next year")
- Rising prices
- Few suppliers
- New capacity that takes years to build

Power and memory fit all of these.

### Step 4: Gather early signals

Set up sources and check them regularly:

- **Everyday observation:** retail prices, product availability, what people in the industry complain about.
- **Online chatter:** Reddit, X, industry forums, Google Trends.
- **Industry data:** price trackers (like TrendForce for memory), trade publications, government data.
- **Company sources:** earnings call transcripts from customers as well as suppliers. A hyperscaler mentioning "power constraints" is a signal for power companies.
- **Deal announcements:** contracts and partnerships, like Bloom's with Oracle and Brookfield.

Keep a journal with dated notes so you can see whether signals are strengthening.

### Step 5: Identify the companies

For the bottleneck you've found, list the public companies that sell into it. Then filter:

- **Materiality:** how much of the company's revenue comes from this? A small company with heavy exposure moves far more than a giant where it's a side business.
- **Pricing power:** can it raise prices, or will competitors undercut it?
- **Ability to deliver:** does it have capacity, or is it also constrained?

### Step 6: Check whether it's priced in

- How far has the stock already run?
- Are analysts and media already covering this story widely?
- Compare valuation to growth. A high multiple means high expectations already built in.
- Read analyst reports or earnings call Q&A. If analysts are asking about it, the gap is closing.

The ideal: signals are strong but coverage is thin.

### Step 7: Find the catalyst

Identify the event that will force the market to notice:

- Earnings reports, where the trend shows up in revenue or margins
- Guidance raises
- Major contract announcements
- Industry price data releases

Positioning before a catalyst is the core of Camillo's approach.

### Step 8: Size and enter the position

- Decide in advance how much you're willing to lose on this idea, often a small percentage of your portfolio.
- Consider entering in stages rather than all at once.
- Choose your vehicle: shares, or options strategies like CSPs, PMCCs or plain calls, depending on conviction and risk tolerance.
- Write down your thesis in a sentence or two: why it will work, and what would prove it wrong.

### Step 9: Monitor the thesis

Keep tracking the same signals that got you in:

- Is demand still growing?
- Are prices still rising, and at what rate?
- Is new supply or competition arriving?
- Is the company delivering in its results?

### Step 10: Know when to exit

Plan exits in advance. Common triggers:

- **The gap closes:** the story is everywhere and the valuation reflects it.
- **Growth decelerates:** prices still rising, but more slowly. Stocks often peak here, especially in cyclical industries like memory.
- **New supply or competitors arrive.**
- **Your thesis breaks:** what you said would prove you wrong happens.
- **Your loss limit is hit.**

## Strategy fit

### LEAPS: high weight

The best fit of the three. A bottleneck typically takes several quarters to resolve, and the upside is uncapped, so a long-dated call gives the shortage time to flow through multiple earnings reports.

- **Use it as a primary selection driver**, filtered hard by Step 5 (materiality) and Step 6 (priced in). A LEAPS on a company where the bottleneck is a side business, or where the multiple already assumes it, is paying for a story that cannot surprise.
- **Prefer the pure plays.** Smaller companies with heavy exposure to the constrained layer move the most. Check the option chain exists and is liquid before you fall in love with the thesis.
- **Watch the deceleration trigger.** In cyclical bottlenecks like memory, the stock often peaks while prices are still rising. Plan to trim a LEAPS when the rate of increase slows, not when prices fall.

### PMCC (poor man's covered call): low to moderate weight

Better suited here than to consumer trends, because a bottleneck tends to produce a grind higher over quarters rather than a single gap-up. That said, the short call still caps the move around each catalyst.

- **Pick PMCC underlyings** among the larger, steadier names in the bottleneck layer, where the re-rating is gradual and the options are liquid.
- **Skip or go far out of the money on the short call** into the catalysts identified in Step 7: earnings, guidance, big contract announcements, industry price releases.
- **Resume selling calls** once Step 10 signals appear, especially when growth decelerates. At that point the capped upside costs less and the premium helps you exit gracefully.

### CSP (cash-secured put): moderate weight, as a screen and an entry tool

The bottleneck thesis answers the real CSP question, "would I own this at the strike?", better than most frameworks do.

- **As an entry method:** Step 8 suggests entering in stages. Selling puts below the market on a company you want to own is a disciplined way to do that, and you collect premium while waiting.
- **As a veto:** if Step 9 shows new supply arriving or prices decelerating, do not sell puts there however rich the premium. Cyclical names fall fast once the shortage eases.
- If your conviction is high and the catalyst is near, a CSP wastes most of the edge. Calls or shares express that view better.

## Rough guide

| Strategy | Weight of the bottleneck thesis | Main role |
|---|---|---|
| LEAPS | High (co-equal with valuation and liquidity) | Picking the stock, holding through several catalysts |
| PMCC | Low to moderate | Steadier large-cap names in the layer; timing the short calls around catalysts |
| CSP | Moderate | Staged entry into names you want to own; veto when supply is arriving |

## Practical cautions

- **Bottlenecks are cyclical.** High prices call forth new supply. Memory, shipping and commodity shortages have all ended this way, and the stocks usually turn before the price data does.
- **Second-order plays are crowded fast.** Once "picks and shovels" becomes the consensus framing, the obvious layers (chips, power) are priced in. The less obvious layers in Step 2 are where thin coverage still exists.
- **Materiality is easy to overstate.** A company can be "in" a bottleneck layer with only a small fraction of revenue exposed. Check the segment numbers, not the press release.
- **The constrained supplier may be constrained too.** Pricing power is worthless if the company cannot ship. Step 5's "ability to deliver" filter is the one most often skipped.
- **Small-cap option chains are often poor.** Spreads can be wide and LEAPS may not exist. Check liquidity before the signal excites you.
- **IV crush and "priced in" risk.** Implied volatility is usually high before earnings. Being right about demand does not protect you from guidance or margin disappointments.
- **Confirmation bias.** A good supply-chain map makes everything look like a bottleneck. Write down what would prove you wrong before you size up, and reread it.

## How OptionsScout implements it

The method is the second market-intelligence signal in this repo, specified in `PRD.md` section 4A and built as Phase 11 (section 14). The mapping of the ten steps onto the system:

| Step | In the app |
|---|---|
| 1 Theme, 2 supply chain, 5 companies | Curated in `config/themes.yaml`: theme → layers → companies with materiality, pricing power and capacity. Tickers are validated against the SEC list when the config loads. |
| 3 Bottleneck, 4 signals | Adapters read SEC EDGAR full-text search, FRED price series, operator-supplied price-tracker CSVs, news RSS and industry subreddits. The LLM classifies each snippet as tightening or easing, customer or supplier. Python computes tightness, customer confirmation, price trend, price deceleration and supply response. |
| 6 Priced in, 7 catalyst | Computed from data the app already fetches: 90-day run vs SPY, distance to the 52-week high, news coverage, IV rank, next earnings date. |
| 8 Position | The existing Portfolio and Risk agents size positions. The strategy-fit ideas above become flags on candidates, never filters: `bottleneck_support`, `bottleneck_easing`, `catalyst_inside_dte`. |
| 9 Monitor, 10 exit | A daily job stores dated snapshots (the journal) and evaluates each layer's written falsifiers. A tripped falsifier halves the score and sets the easing flag. The app reports the state of the thesis and never uses exit language. |

Two constraints carry over from the consumer-trend signal. The score only **re-orders stocks that already passed the good-to-own gate**, so a small-cap pure play appears on the board as context and is never screened unless the user adds it and it passes the gate. And the two signals together are **capped at 15% of the ranking**, so a shortage can move Honeywell ahead of Union Pacific but cannot put a speculative name on the list.

## Sources and basis for this logic

### Standard options mechanics

These points are textbook and verifiable in any serious options text:

- A covered call or PMCC short call caps your upside; a short put's profit is capped at the premium.
- Implied volatility tends to rise before earnings and collapse after (IV crush).
- Long-dated options cost more in time value.

References:
- Lawrence McMillan, *Options as a Strategic Investment*
- Sheldon Natenberg, *Option Volatility and Pricing*
- OCC, *Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options*
- tastytrade, which popularized the term "poor man's covered call" and has free material on PMCCs and CSPs

### Origins of the approach

- **Chris Camillo**, whose "social arbitrage" method (*Laughing at Wall Street*) supplies the signal-gathering and positioning-before-a-catalyst discipline in Steps 4 and 7. The bottleneck framing extends his consumer-facing approach to industrial supply chains.
- **Supply-chain and "picks and shovels" investing** is an older idea: during a boom, sell the tools rather than dig for gold. Its modern form in the AI buildout (power, memory, cooling, networking) is the running example throughout this document.
- **Industry data sources** named in the steps, such as TrendForce for memory pricing, are the kind of trackers that make Step 4 concrete.

### Related evidence for the underlying edge

- Research on alternative data and attention (see the citations in `consumer-trends.md`) supports the general claim that information not yet in consensus estimates predicts earnings surprises, and that the edge decays as the information spreads.
- The deceleration trigger in Step 10 reflects a widely observed pattern in cyclical industries: stock prices tend to peak when the second derivative of pricing or volume turns, well before the first derivative does.

*Citations and company examples (Bloom, Oracle, Brookfield) are given from memory and have not been re-verified.*

### What is judgment, not established finding

The ten-step process itself is a structured checklist, not a tested system. The strategy weightings (high for LEAPS, low to moderate for PMCC, moderate for CSP) and the mapping of specific steps onto specific option tactics are a synthesis of the two bodies of knowledge above. No known study tests bottleneck investing across these three option structures, so treat that part as a reasoned framework rather than a proven result.
